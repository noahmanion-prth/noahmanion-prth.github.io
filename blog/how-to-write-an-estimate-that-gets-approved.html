How to write an estimate that gets approved

Send it fast, make the scope impossible to misread, put the deposit and terms on the page, and make yes the easiest answer. A checklist for contractors.

By The MX Build team · October 13, 2026· 5 min read

In this article
  1. Send it while they're still thinking about it
  2. Make the scope impossible to misread
  3. Show the price the way they think about it
  4. Put the deposit and the terms on the page
  5. Make yes the easiest thing to do
  6. Follow up at the right moment
  7. A quick checklist before you hit send

Most estimates don't lose on price. They lose because they showed up late, said too little, or made the customer do work to say yes.

You can't control what the other contractors charge. You can control how fast your estimate arrives, how clear it is, and how easy it is to approve. Here's how to get each of those right.

Send it while they're still thinking about it

The best time to get a yes is right after you've walked the job. The customer just watched you look at the problem. They've heard you explain it. They trust you more right now than they will tomorrow.

Every hour after you leave, that fades. They call someone else. They talk to a neighbor. They start to wonder if the job is really necessary.

So send the estimate before you leave the driveway, or as close to it as you can. If that means building it on your phone instead of at the kitchen table that night, do that. A clear estimate today beats a perfect one on Thursday.

To make that possible, have your common jobs priced ahead of time. If you're building every line from scratch, you'll always put it off until you're at a desk.

Make the scope impossible to misread

The scope is the part customers argue about later. "I thought that included the trim." "You didn't say anything about hauling the old one away." Every one of those fights starts with a vague estimate.

Write the scope so someone who wasn't there could read it and know exactly what you'll do:

  • Use separate line items for separate pieces of work. "Remove and haul away old water heater" is its own line, not buried in "install."
  • Be specific about materials. "50 gal gas water heater, 6-year warranty" says more than "water heater."
  • Say what's not included. Drywall repair after you open a wall, permit fees if the customer pulls them, painting after electrical work. Write down the stuff people assume.
  • Note your assumptions. "Price assumes existing gas line is to code." If that turns out wrong, you've already told them a change is possible.

This doesn't need to be long. It needs to be specific.

Show the price the way they think about it

A single lump sum is hard to say yes to. The customer can't see what they're paying for, so the number feels arbitrary, and the only way to push back is to ask for a discount.

Line items fix that. When the customer sees "Permit fee: $120" as its own line, they understand it's not your markup. When they see the labor separate from the materials, they can see where the money goes.

For bigger jobs, consider giving options. Two or three versions of the same job at different price points let the customer choose between your options instead of choosing between you and someone else. A basic repair, a full replacement, and a replacement with an upgrade is a common setup. Keep it to three at most, and make the differences easy to see.

Put the deposit and the terms on the page

If you need a deposit, say so on the estimate. Don't spring it on them after they say yes.

Spell out:

  • How much is due to start, as a dollar amount, not just a percent.
  • What the deposit covers. "Deposit covers ordering equipment" makes it feel reasonable.
  • When the balance is due. On completion? Within a set number of days?
  • How long the price is good for. Material prices move. Thirty days is common. Pick what works for your trade.
  • How you handle changes. "Changes to the scope will be quoted and approved before work begins."

Clear terms make you look like a business, and they head off almost every payment argument before it starts.

Make yes the easiest thing to do

This is where a lot of good estimates stall. The customer wants to go ahead, but they have to print it, sign it, scan it, and email it back. Or call you during work hours. Or remember to text you later. So they don't, and a week goes by.

Make approving one step. If they can read it on their phone, tap approve, and pay the deposit in the same place, they'll often do it right then.

At the bottom of the estimate, say exactly what happens next: "Approve this estimate and pay the deposit to get on the schedule. We'll confirm your start date within one business day." (Use a promise you can keep.)

Follow up at the right moment

Following up isn't pushy. It's part of the job. Customers get busy, and an estimate that sits unopened for three days usually isn't a no. It's just buried.

The best time to follow up is when you know they're looking at it. If your estimates tell you when they've been opened, call shortly after. You're not interrupting. They're already thinking about it.

If you can't tell, a short text a day or two later works: "Hi, just checking you got the estimate for the water heater. Happy to answer any questions." One question, no pressure.

If they've opened it several times and haven't approved, something's holding them up. Price, timing, or a question they haven't asked. A quick call usually finds out what.

A quick checklist before you hit send

  • Sent the same day as the walkthrough
  • Every piece of work is its own line item
  • Materials are specific
  • What's not included is written down
  • Assumptions are noted
  • Options, if the job has them, are clear and limited to three
  • Deposit amount and what it covers
  • When the balance is due
  • How long the price is good for
  • How changes are handled
  • One clear next step to approve

Run through that list every time and your estimates will look more professional than most of what your customers get.
