How to price a plumbing service call

Work out what an hour really costs you, decide what the fee covers, and say the number before you roll the truck. A practical guide for plumbing shops.

By The MX Build team · October 14, 2026· 5 min read

In this article

  2. Decide what the fee covers
  3. Use flat-rate pricing for the repair
  4. Charge more for nights, weekends, and emergencies
  5. Say the number before you go
  6. Check your numbers every few months
  7. The short version

A service call fee pays for one thing: getting a licensed plumber and a stocked truck to someone's door. Price it too low and every small job loses money before you pick up a wrench. Price it without a plan and you end up arguing about it on the porch.

Here's how to set a service call price you can defend, and how to charge it without the awkward conversation.

Start with what an hour actually costs you

Most plumbers price service calls by looking at what the shop down the road charges. That tells you what the market will bear. It doesn't tell you whether you're making money.

Start with your own numbers. Add up a year of everything it takes to put a plumber in front of a customer:

  • Labor. Wages for everyone in the field, plus payroll taxes, workers' comp, and any benefits.
  • Trucks. Payments or depreciation, fuel, insurance, maintenance, and tires.
  • Insurance and licensing. General liability, bonding, license renewals, and continuing education.
  • Tools and equipment. Camera, jetter, press tools, and the replacements you buy every year.
  • Office. Phones, software, accounting, advertising, rent, and whoever answers the phone. If that's you, count your hours too.

Now figure out how many hours your techs actually bill in a year. Not hours on the clock. Hours you charge a customer for. Drive time, supply house runs, callbacks, and estimates that don't close all come out.

Divide total cost by billable hours. That's your break-even hour. Everything you charge has to clear it, and your profit sits on top.

Here's an example with round numbers, not real ones. Say a one-truck shop spends $180,000 a year to keep the truck on the road and the owner paid, and bills 1,200 hours. Break-even is $150 an hour. If a service call takes an hour door to door, charging $99 for it means losing $51 every time the phone rings.

Your numbers will be different. The point is to know yours.

Decide what the fee covers

"Service call" means different things at different shops. Pick one and write it down, so everyone on the crew says the same thing.

The common setups:

  • Trip charge. Covers getting there. Diagnosis and repair are priced separately. Easy to explain, but customers sometimes feel nickel-and-dimed when the next number shows up.
  • Diagnostic fee. Covers getting there and finding the problem. You give the repair price after you know what's wrong.
  • Service call that includes time. Covers the trip and a set amount of work, like the first 30 minutes. Simple jobs are done for one price. Bigger ones get quoted.
  • Fee applied to the repair. You charge the fee, then take it off the repair price if they go ahead. This helps close jobs, but only if the repair price has room for it.

There's no right answer. What matters is that the fee covers the cost of the visit even when the customer says no to the repair. If it doesn't, you're paying to give free estimates.

Use flat-rate pricing for the repair

Once you're on site, you can price the repair two ways.

Time and materials bills hours plus parts. It's fair when the job is truly unknown, like chasing a slab leak. But the customer has to trust you not to drag your feet, and your fastest tech makes the least money.

Flat rate prices the task, not the hours. A toilet rebuild is a set price whether it takes 40 minutes or 70. Customers know the number before you start, and your good techs are rewarded for being good.

To build a flat-rate price for a task:

  1. Estimate the typical time, including setup and cleanup.
  2. Multiply by your billable hour rate (break-even plus profit).
  3. Add parts at your marked-up price.
  4. Round to a clean number.

Do this for the 20 or 30 jobs you run most: drain clears, faucet swaps, toilet rebuilds, disposal installs, water heater replacements, shutoff valves. Save them in one price book so every tech quotes the same job at the same price. That's what keeps the new guy from undercutting you by a hundred bucks.

Charge more for nights, weekends, and emergencies

A burst pipe at 11 p.m. costs you more than the same pipe at 10 a.m. You're pulling someone off their night, paying overtime, and maybe skipping tomorrow's first job.

Set a separate after-hours rate instead of making it up on the phone. Two common ways:

  • A separate after-hours service call fee, with normal flat rates for the repair.
  • A multiplier on the whole ticket, like time and a half.

Whichever you pick, define "after hours" once. Weeknights after 6? Weekends? Holidays? Put it in writing so the customer and the tech both know.

Say the number before you go

The fight over a service call fee almost never happens because the fee is too high. It happens because the customer didn't know about it until the invoice.

Tell them on the phone: "The service call is $X. That covers getting a plumber out and finding the problem. Once we know what's wrong, we'll give you a price before we do any work."

Then send it in writing. A text or an estimate they can open on their phone, with the service call fee as its own line. If you can get them to approve it before you roll, even better. Now the fee isn't a surprise at the door. It's something they already said yes to.

This matters most for after-hours calls, when people are stressed and less likely to remember what you told them.

Check your numbers every few months

Costs change. Fuel goes up, a tech gets a raise, insurance renews higher. A service call fee you set two years ago may not cover the visit anymore.

Every quarter or so, look at:

  • Your break-even hour. Has any big cost changed?
  • Calls that didn't turn into work. If you're running a lot of them, the fee needs to carry more of the load.
  • Your most common flat-rate tasks. Are techs finishing in the time you assumed? If a job keeps running long, the price is wrong, or the task needs to be split.

Small adjustments, made regularly, beat a big price jump that surprises your repeat customers.

The short version

  1. Work out your break-even hour from real costs and real billable hours.
  2. Decide what your service call covers, and make sure it pays for the visit on its own.
  3. Price repairs flat-rate from a shared price book.
  4. Set an after-hours rate and define when it applies.
  5. Tell the customer the fee before you go, and put it in writing.
  6. Recheck the numbers every few months.
