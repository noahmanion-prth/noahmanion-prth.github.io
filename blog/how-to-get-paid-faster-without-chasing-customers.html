How to get paid faster without chasing customers

Agree on payment before the work, take deposits on bigger jobs, invoice the day you finish, and make paying the easiest thing they do today.

By The MX Build team · October 12, 2026· 4 min read

In this article

  2. Take a deposit on bigger jobs
  3. Invoice the day the job's done
  4. Make paying the easiest thing they do today
  5. Bill change orders as you go
  6. When it's late, follow up like a business
  7. Know what's out at all times
  8. The short version

Doing the work is the part you're good at. Getting paid for it shouldn't be a second job.

Most late payments aren't customers trying to stiff you. They're customers who weren't told when to pay, got the invoice a week late, or found it hard to pay. Fix those, and most of the chasing goes away. Here's how.

Agree on payment before the work starts

The easiest payment conversation happens before you've done anything. Once the work is finished, you've lost your leverage, and the customer has lost their urgency.

Put your payment terms on the estimate, where the customer sees them before they say yes:

  • The deposit, if there is one, and when it's due.
  • When the balance is due. "Due on completion" is clear. "Net 30" invites the customer to wait 30 days.
  • How they can pay. If you take cards, say so. If you don't take checks, say that too.
  • What happens if it's late. If you charge late fees, they have to be in your terms before the job. Check the rules where you work.

When the customer approves the estimate, they're agreeing to all of it. Now the invoice isn't a surprise. It's what they already agreed to.

Take a deposit on bigger jobs

A deposit does three things. It covers your materials so you're not floating the cost. It shows the customer is serious. And it gets money moving before the job is done, which makes the final payment feel like the last step instead of the whole bill.

How much to ask for depends on your trade and the job. A common rule is enough to cover materials. For a water heater swap, that's the tank and the parts. For a paint job, it's the paint and supplies. For a panel upgrade, it's the panel and the wire.

Ask for it in the same step as the approval if you can. "Approve and pay the deposit to get on the schedule" turns two decisions into one.

Invoice the day the job's done

The longer you wait to send the invoice, the longer you wait to get paid. That sounds obvious, but a lot of shops still batch invoices on Friday night or at the end of the month.

The moment the job is done is the moment the customer is happiest with you. The leak is fixed. The lights work. Ask for payment then, while they're standing in a room that works.

If your estimate is already written, the invoice should be a few taps, not a retyping session. Any approved changes should already be on it.

Make paying the easiest thing they do today

Every step between the customer and paying you is a chance to put it off. Printing a check, finding a stamp, logging into a portal they've never used, calling you with a card number. Each one adds days.

The fewest steps wins. A link they can open on their phone, see the total, and pay right there. No app to download, no account to make.

If you still take checks, that's fine. Just don't make it the only option. The customer who would pay by card tonight might not get to the bank until next week.

Bill change orders as you go

Change orders are where a lot of payment fights start. You did extra work because the customer asked for it, or because you opened the wall and found a problem. Then the invoice comes in higher than the estimate and the customer balks.

The fix is to get every change approved before you do the work:

  1. Stop and explain what you found or what changed.
  2. Send a written change order with the added cost.
  3. Get it approved before you keep going.

Now the final invoice is the estimate plus changes they already said yes to. There's nothing to argue about.

When it's late, follow up like a business

Some invoices will still go past due. Have a plan, so you're not deciding what to do every time:

  • A day or two after the due date: a friendly reminder. "Hi, just a reminder that the invoice for the furnace repair is due. Here's the link to pay."
  • About a week late: a call. Ask if there's a problem with the work or the bill. Sometimes there is, and it's better to know.
  • Two to three weeks late: a firm written notice that restates the amount, the due date, and any late fee from your terms.
  • After that: decide whether it's worth a collections agency or small claims court. Know the rules in your area before you go there.

Keep every message short, polite, and factual. You're not angry. You're running a business, and this is the process.

Know what's out at all times

You can't follow up on invoices you've forgotten about. Once a week, look at everything that's sent but not paid. Sort it by how late it is. Make the reminders and calls right then.

It takes 15 minutes, and it's the difference between catching a late invoice at one week and noticing it at three months.

The short version

  1. Put payment terms on the estimate, before the work starts.
  2. Take a deposit on bigger jobs, in the same step as the approval.
  3. Invoice the day the job is done.
  4. Let customers pay from a link on their phone.
  5. Get change orders approved before you do the extra work.
  6. Follow a set schedule when an invoice is late.
  7. Review what's outstanding every week.
